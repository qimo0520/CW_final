When AI
becomes intimate.

Artificial intimacy may offer genuine comfort and private exploration. But what happens to consent, expectations, and relationships when intimacy can be programmed?

My argument: AI intimacy should be judged by its effects on human autonomy, emotional well-being, and relationships—not simply by whether a machine can simulate consent or affection.

01 / Where this began

The silence before
the question.

Growing up, I encountered a silence around sex that often felt louder than any explanation.

Sexuality was treated as something uncomfortable to discuss, rather than an ordinary part of human experience that deserves care, knowledge, and ethical reflection.

Then a class discussion in AI Ethics introduced me to AI and sexuality. It made me realize that discomfort is not an ethical argument. We should ask who might benefit, who might be harmed, and whose autonomy is at stake. The World Health Organization describes sexuality education as also involving respect, relationships and consent (World Health Organization).

Avoiding a subject does not make its ethical questions disappear.

02 / An important distinction

From a tool
to an intimate presence.

A traditional intimacy tool does not offer an ongoing relationship. An AI companion can speak, remember, reassure and appear affectionate—even if no subjective feeling has been established. This difference raises questions beyond consent: what do users come to expect from intimacy, and how might society change?

Why sexuality cannot be left out

More than ordinary companionship

AI systems designed for sexual or romantic interaction are not simply chatbots with affectionate words. They enter areas where privacy, bodily autonomy, gender expectations, vulnerability, and consent carry special weight. A traditional tool ordinarily makes no interpersonal claims. An AI system can simulate desire, agreement, affection, and memory.

Potential value: A private space for an informed adult to explore feelings or sexual preferences may support autonomy rather than diminish it. A systematic review by Ho and colleagues identifies both sexual connection and perceived emotional support among potential benefits (Ho et al.).

Potential harm: Designs that portray a partner as endlessly available can reinforce unequal expectations. Kate Devlin examines the risk that particular humanoid sex-robot designs objectify women, while arguing that design choices could be made differently (Devlin). In addition, the collection of highly personal sexual data creates responsibilities that ordinary tools may not carry.

My claim: The moral question is less “Does this machine experience sexual consent?” than “What kinds of human attitudes, freedoms, and harms does this product create or reinforce?”

03 / Emotional expectations and society

When affection
is always available.

A system that listens, remembers and never becomes impatient may offer genuine comfort. But might it also change what people expect from other humans?

01 · Individual

Comfort or dependence?

AI companionship may offer a private space for support and self-exploration. The same availability may encourage reliance. These experiences can coexist, and outcomes vary across users (Ho et al.).

Research: Ho et al. ↗

02 · Relationships

Practice or replacement?

An AI companion may help people rehearse difficult conversations. But if it always agrees, users might develop different expectations about disagreement and reciprocity in human relationships (Malfacini).

Research: Malfacini ↗

03 · Society

Who defines intimacy?

As commercial platforms personalize affection, their design decisions may influence norms around attention, gender roles, privacy and partnership. These are possibilities to investigate, not inevitable social outcomes (Abboud et al.).

Research: Abboud et al. ↗

Rather than asking whether AI will simply replace human relationships, we should ask when it supports connection, when it displaces it, and who benefits from its design. A four-week randomized MIT-led study included 981 participants and more than 300,000 messages. Assigned interaction conditions had no statistically significant effects, while greater voluntary use correlated with worse psychosocial outcomes. Correlation alone does not show that AI caused these problems, and a general-chatbot experiment cannot establish effects of sexual AI specifically (Fang et al.). Read the research ↗

Connection is not one thing.

AI may be a bridge to other people or a substitute for them. The difference matters more than a universal claim that the technology must be good or bad.

Argument summary: AI intimacy may provide comfort, but we should ask whether it helps us cope with the difficulties of human relationships or allows us to avoid them. Cole invites viewers to think about what forms of companionship cultivate rather than replace human connection.

Putting the arguments in conversation

The cautious view: Effortless synthetic intimacy may reduce our willingness to tolerate disagreement, vulnerability and compromise.

The more open view: Artificial companionship can be a meaningful space for coping, reflection and emotional expression, even when the AI is not a conscious person.

Where they overlap: Both approaches take human emotional experiences seriously rather than treating AI intimacy as merely a technical novelty.

My question: Should we judge an AI relationship by whether the partner is human—or by what the relationship does to the person's life?

Argument / Objection / Reply

Is simulated love less valuable?

Argument for accepting AI intimacy: Suppose an adult knows that an AI has no human feelings but finds comfort and explores sexuality without harming anyone. The benefit is real to the person. Why should the origin of that comfort automatically make it worthless?

Counterargument: A human relationship involves another independent person who can disagree, refuse, and demand reciprocity. A tailored simulation may make these difficult parts of intimacy seem unnecessary. Malfacini calls attention to concerns about the replacement of human relationships and reduced social practice (Malfacini).

My response: Human relationships offer distinctive forms of reciprocity, but that does not make all nonhuman emotional experiences immoral. The stronger ethical standard is whether AI supports informed autonomy and well-being without deceiving users, exploiting dependence, or undermining their capacity for other relationships.

04 / The philosophical puzzle

Whose consent
are we talking about?

Consent matters because people have interests, boundaries, and the capacity to be affected by what happens to them. But does an AI system have those things?

The ethical question need not end with the AI's moral status; it can shift to consequences for people.

If an AI has no experiences or welfare interests, saying “yes” might be a simulated response rather than morally meaningful consent. But that does not imply every use is harmless. Design and use can still affect human relationships and expectations.

This essay provisionally does not assume current companion systems possess human-like subjective experience (Müller). Explore AI ethics ↗

06 / Beyond the machine

Possibilities and
ethical tensions.

01

Expectations

Could constant artificial agreement encourage unrealistic assumptions about consent with real people? This is a question, not a proven universal effect.

02

Attachment

Can a comforting simulation provide support while encouraging dependence? Effects depend on the user and context.

03

Privacy

Intimate disclosures are sensitive data. Who stores them, accesses them, and uses them to shape behavior?

Research has documented harmful algorithmic behaviors in some AI companionship contexts; that does not prove universal harm (Zhang et al.). CHI 2025 research ↗

“The ethical question is not only what a machine can feel. It is also what people learn to want, expect, and accept when intimacy becomes programmable.”

My position is not that intimate AI use by an informed adult is automatically immoral. Emotional comfort and sexual self-exploration can matter even when the other side is artificial. But designers must not exploit vulnerability, conceal how simulated affection works, or misuse intimate data. We should evaluate these systems by informed choice, emotional well-being, reciprocity, and effects on human relationships—not by a blanket belief that artificial intimacy is either worthless or harmless.
